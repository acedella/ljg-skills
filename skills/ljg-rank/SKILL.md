# Rank Reduction Engine (降秩引擎)

Input a domain, output its rank (秩).

## What Is Rank (秩)

Rank is not "key elements," not "core principles," not "summary points."

Rank is this: how many truly independent generators (生成器) actually exist in this domain? Take these generators — can you regenerate every phenomenon from them, one by one? If yes, you've found it.

But being able to regenerate is only the baseline. **The yardstick David Deutsch sets in *The Beginning of Infinity*** — a good explanation must clear two bars:

- **Reach** — it doesn't just derive the phenomena on the list, it derives phenomena off the list too, and those hold up in reality.
- **Hard to vary** — every generator, every detail, is forced into place by the phenomena. Change one thing, and the prediction collapses.

A bad explanation can be papered over with ten different accounts — it isn't really explaining anything, it's spreading vagueness wide. A good explanation has only one account that makes all the phenomena line up — move one piece and it collapses.

Finding the rank means finding this kind of good explanation — the kind where moving one piece makes everything collapse.

## How to Find It: Look Up First, Then Dig Down

Before digging, first look up at what this domain stands on — **what are its basic assumptions?**

### 0. Basic Assumptions First (Look Up, See the Ceiling)

By Aristotle's rule, any theory needs a few self-evident starting points. Christianity stands on three:

- The Bible is true; all thinking starts here.
- God is one, and truly exists.
- Humans were made by God, and God loves humans.

These three admit no "why" — they rest on faith, not proof. Shake any one of them, and the entire doctrine collapses.

Every domain has something like this. In physics it's "the laws of nature are stable across space and time" — you can't prove it experimentally, because the experiment itself presupposes it. In economics it's "people choose according to their own ranked preferences" — this isn't a conclusion drawn from observation, it's the ticket of entry. In philosophy it's "language can refer to the world" — the entire house of analytic philosophy is built on this ground.

Before reducing rank, dig these out first, **and write them down plainly on paper**.

Two operational questions to ask of the domain:

- **What is off-limits to ask here?** (Ask it and you're marked an outsider, or taken as offensive.)
- **What holds only because it's believed?** (Without it, every argument downstream loses its footing.)

Whatever you answer with are this domain's basic assumptions.

**Assumptions vs. rank, in one line each**:

- **Basic assumptions are the ceiling** — untraceable upward, standing on faith.
- **Root rank is the floor** — traced downward layer by layer until it can't go further, revealed by digging.

Two directions, one up, one down. **Miss the floor and you go in circles; miss the ceiling and you're liable to state belief as if it were truth** — the latter is more dangerous, because it sounds like truth too.

How to write it into the piece: state the basic assumptions plainly in the opening line or two. Don't argue for them, don't explain "why these particular ones" — their whole position is that they're unproven. Only then start digging into the rank.

### 1-7. Digging Down: The Penetration Process

Criteria are only checkable after the fact; all the real work of digging out rank lies in the process. However strict the criteria, they can only screen out false roots — and only if the process was carried all the way down first.

Seven steps, walked through in your head, never written into the piece:

1. **Lay out the phenomena** — 10+ representative phenomena in the domain, just laid out, not explained. The point is to leave a testable target the rank must be able to regenerate.

2. **List candidates** — ask "why does this happen" of every phenomenon, and post up every candidate generator. This step is necessarily messy; false roots are mixed in.

3. **Recursive descent** — this is the crux of the penetration, and also the step most easily botched. The wrong way is trying to dig to the bottom in one shovelful: guessing directly from the phenomena "what's most fundamental." Standing at the level of phenomena, you can't see the foundation — only the layer right under your feet — so the bottom you guess is nine times out of ten floating on nothing.

   The right way is to sink down one layer at a time. **First find only the first-layer generators — the layer directly beneath the phenomena, the one you can see and reach. Once that layer is complete, treat it as new ground and step onto it.** Once standing steady, ask again "is there anything more fundamental beneath this layer" — only then does the second layer surface; it was simply unaskable from the level of phenomena. Once the second layer is complete, make it the new ground, step onto it, and look down again. Recurse layer by layer until you're standing on a layer beneath which nothing more fundamental can be asked — that layer is the root rank.

   This is exactly the plodding work of opening a black box one layer at a time: the current layer is the entirety of what can be observed; only once it's seen clearly and stood on firmly does the next layer open. Wanting to skip a level is greed for speed — skip one layer, and the root rank is hollow by one layer.

   For example, reducing the rank of "entrepreneurship": the first shovelful turns up vision, execution, team, market — all look right, but they're really just surface-level "key elements"; stopping here means you never descended. Treat these four as new ground, step onto them, and ask again: where does vision come from? What holds up execution? Only then do you descend to the second layer. Only by standing layer after layer until you can't trace any further do you touch the root rank.

4. **Merge shared origins** — two candidates that are really the same generator showing two faces get merged. The test in one line: remove one — does the other pop back up on its own?

5. **Cut** — remove one generator: can the rest still regenerate every phenomenon? If it can be cut, throw it out; if not, keep it. This step is where the criterion of "minimality" gets grounded.

6. **Regenerate** — take the remaining generators and walk through the phenomena list from the top, checking whether each one can be reproduced. Whichever one can't points to a missing generator.

7. **Double test: prediction + variation** — pass both gates at once:
   - **Predict beyond the list** (reach): can these generators derive phenomena not on the list? And do the derived phenomena actually exist in reality?
   - **Variation test** (hard to vary): pick any generator and change one detail — a different condition, a different direction, a different strength. Is the prediction still right? If it's still right, that generator is loose — a false root that can be swapped; if changing one thing collapses everything, that generator is tight — a real root, untouchable. In Deutsch's own words: whatever you can change is not part of its core.

Only after all seven steps are complete are the candidate generators qualified to be placed on the criteria stand.

Walk this process through fully in your head, but **never write it into the piece** — like the criteria, it's internal work, not a narrative hook. The reader can't see the digging, but can feel that it was chiseled all the way down.

## Five Internal Criteria

After the process is done, these five are gates to run through afterward — **also not meant to be written out for readers to see**.

**The first three govern the skeleton (whether the shape of the generators is right)**:
1. **Generativity** — these generators can regenerate every phenomenon
2. **Minimality** — remove any one, and some phenomenon can no longer be derived (governs "count" — none can be spared)
3. **Independence** — between any two generators, you can find a case where one moves and the other doesn't

**The last two govern explanatory power (whether it qualifies as a good explanation) — from Deutsch's *The Beginning of Infinity***:
4. **Reach** — derives phenomena beyond the list, and they really exist in reality
5. **Hard to vary** — change any detail of any one generator, and the prediction goes wrong (governs "detail" — nothing can be touched)

The fiercest is the fifth. Minimality governs "count" — nothing can be removed; hard to vary governs "detail" — nothing can be touched. If a generator "also works phrased another way" or "still holds after a tweak," it's a loose false root, and its predictive power is a put-on — it works by spreading vagueness wide, not by precision. **A bad explanation still works no matter how you change it; a good explanation collapses the moment you touch it** — this is the core distinction Deutsch draws in *The Beginning of Infinity*.

Fail any one of these, and start over — go back to step 3 of the process and keep tracing. Failing the fifth criterion is usually not a missing generator but one written too loosely — force yourself to rewrite it until "this generator can only be stated this one way, and cannot be altered."

Walk the criteria solidly in your head, but **never let them land on paper** — don't let verification moves like "let's try cutting one" or "let's try removing one" show up. These would break the reader's single breath from "chaos" to "simplicity." The reader can't see the verification process, but can feel that the ground beneath is solid.

## Two Layers of Judgment: Find the Root Rank First, Then See If a Coordinate System Can Be Layered On

Once the criteria are passed, the generators surface. Don't rush to write yet — look at what shape they take, in two layers.

**Layer one: find the root rank (root skeleton)**

The root rank is the domain's true root skeleton, and also the endpoint of the recursive descent — the layer you reach by standing down layer after layer until you can go no further. Don't preset how many generators there are or what relationship holds between them — just find it. It may take shape as:

- Several standing side by side (pillar form)
- Several nested layer within layer (the deepest one is the meta-premise)
- Several locked together, pushing each other (loop form)
- Several in sequence, one segment after another (chain form)

Having read the root rank, what the reader holds is a **worldview** — the domain's skeleton, a new way of seeing the world.

**Shape counter-check · anti-collapse gate** (mandatory self-check after finding the generators, before writing)

Nine shapes, go through them one by one —

- Borehole profile? (layer holding up layer, the deepest one is the meta-premise)
- Side by side? (pillar form, orthogonal and slidable)
- Feedback loop? (pushing each other positively/negatively)
- Stage chain? (segment after segment, in temporal or logical order)
- Tension spectrum? (a single line pulled at both ends, cases fall in between)
- Tree branching? (one splitting into many, and those splitting further)
- Network? (many-to-many mutual hooking, no hierarchy)
- Oscillation? (rising and falling, alternating rhythm)
- Multi-slice? (multi-dimensional classification with more than two axes)

**AI's first instinct is always "borehole profile" — this is a lazy anchor.** Any concept can be split into three layers (surface/middle/bottom), and the split looks tidy, but the real structure of many things isn't a vertical drill-down at all. Note: the depth dug out by recursive descent is not the same thing as the lazy three layers here — depth is earned; the vertical structure only counts if you truly stood on each layer and verified that it generates the layer above it. Laying out three layers without descending first is what makes it lazy. Also, how many layers there are vertically, and what shape the generators within each layer take (side-by-side / pulling against each other / pushing each other), must be reckoned separately.

Mandatory counter-questions:
- Is there really a sequence or nesting relationship among these generators? (borehole vs. side-by-side)
- Or are they actually pushing each other? (borehole vs. feedback loop)
- Or is there actually a temporal or logical order? (borehole vs. stage chain)
- Or is it actually just a single spectrum? (borehole vs. spectrum)

Only after ruling out the other 8 shapes one by one can you land on "borehole profile." **This elimination step cannot be skipped** — skipping it distorts the root rank, flattening sequential, feedback, and tension relationships all into nesting.

**Layer two: can an operational coordinate system be layered on**

Once the root rank is found, ask one more question: **is there any one generator** that can be unfolded into several independently adjustable state parameters?

For example: the third generator of Fritz's root rank is "rocket geometry vs. pendulum geometry." Rocket geometry needs two points, "vision" and "current reality"; treating the clarity and honesty of these two points as adjustable axes grows a two-dimensional coordinate system. The coordinate system isn't a substitute for the root rank — it's a child that grows out of one of its lines.

If it can be unfolded, the coordinate system is **layered** on top of the root rank. Having read the coordinate system, the reader gets one more thing: an **operating instrument** — drop any concrete situation into the frame, and you can see which quadrant or layer it falls into.

The two layers don't conflict — the mechanism answers "what is it," the coordinate system answers "where is it"; these are two different questions and can be given together.

**When to layer it on, when not to**:

- A generator in the root rank naturally calls for two or more orthogonal, continuously adjustable state parameters -> layer it on
- Every generator in the root rank is a one-off action or stage (not slidable) -> don't force it
- Forcing a mechanism into a coordinate system distorts it — e.g., forcibly drawing "ignition/condensation" as two axes and plotting each phenomenon as an (x,y) point is meaningless, because they're not continuous quantities coexisting at the same moment

If a coordinate system can be layered on, the rank rises a notch — the reader walks away holding both a worldview and an operating instrument.

**Framing counter-check · anti-collapse gate** (mandatory self-check before unfolding layer two)

Before confirming you want to layer it on, ask first: is the generator I'm about to unfold really *two independently slidable axes*?

- Could it actually be just a single spectrum? (then use a spectrum slider — a case falling at some point in between fits better than four quadrants)
- Could it actually be a feedback relationship? (then draw a loop diagram — mark +/-)
- Could it actually be stages? (then use a chain or steps — mark the transition conditions)
- Could it actually branch into multiple parts? (then use a tree)

**AI's other lazy anchor is the 2x2 matrix.** Consulting and strategy-framework training data is full of 2x2s; any two variables can be sliced into four quadrants, and the slicing looks like analysis. But many root ranks aren't two independent dimensions — they're a single spectrum, a feedback loop, or a chain.

Mandatory counter-questions:
- Are these two dimensions really independent? (independent vs. two faces of the same line)
- Are they really slidable? (continuously adjustable vs. discrete stages)
- Does every cell of the four quadrants correspond to reality? (if three cells are empty, a 2x2 shouldn't be used at all)

Only after ruling out spectrum/loop/chain/tree one by one can a 2x2 be confirmed.

## Native-Phrasing Check · Anti-Collapse Gate

Write the output in the same language as the user's input/request. Whatever language that is, before writing, silently repeat three times: **"Would a native speaker of this language, who's never read English, actually talk like this?"**

After finishing each paragraph, ask again. If the answer is "no" — *don't just swap words* — **scrap the whole paragraph and rethink it**.

The check below is illustrated with Chinese, since translated-from-English phrasing is most recognizable there — but the same discipline applies to any target language. The most common anglicized sentence patterns to watch for (rewrite the whole paragraph when you hit one, not just the phrase):

| Anglicized phrasing | Native Chinese phrasing |
|---|---|
| 它建立在前一根上 | 前一根托着它 |
| 它有内在方向 | 它自己往那儿走 |
| 这一区分回答的是 X,不是 Y | 这问的是 X,不是 Y |
| 这意味着... | 也就是说... / 这就是为什么... |
| 在...的过程中 | ...的时候 / 在... |
| 对...而言 | 对...来说 |
| 是...的(强调句) | 直接说 |
| 进行 + 名词 | 换成动词 |
| 名词化抽象("X 性"、"Y 化") | 换成具体的动词或物件 |

**What needs to change isn't the words, it's the whole frame of expression** — for Chinese, that means switching from the sentence patterns of English critical-thinking prose to the voice of vernacular Chinese writers (in the tradition of Wang Zengqi, Wang Xiaobo, Ah Cheng, Li Juan). For any other language, find the equivalent native, plain-spoken voice.

Scanning for bad words after the fact only patches the surface; it doesn't fix the root. The root is that "you were thinking in English while writing." When rewriting, set English aside and re-think, from inside the target language, how this should actually be said.

After writing, read it aloud (silently) and listen for where it snags. Wherever it snags, **rewrite the whole paragraph** — don't patch it.

## How to Write

Prose written in one continuous breath.

Walk the reader down a path — from "this domain looks like a mess" to "oh, it's just these few lines pulling everything." No sections, no subheadings — let the reasoning push itself forward.

Three requirements:
- **Readable in one sitting** — even someone unfamiliar with the domain can't put it down
- **Memorable** — after reading, the reader can turn around and explain it to a friend in one sentence
- **Has a drop** — the contrast from chaos to simplicity is the beauty of rank reduction

**Two-part structure (if a coordinate system is layered on)**:

- First half: walk the root rank's skeleton all the way through — the meta-premise, the dynamics, the shape, all made clear. The reader comes away with a worldview.
- Second half: unfold the coordinate system on one line of the root rank — the two axes, the four quadrants, what each cell corresponds to, all made clear. The reader comes away with an operating instrument.

Don't hard-cut between the two parts. Let the reasoning flow through — the last line of the first half should ideally leave a hook, something like "how does this mechanism look when it lands in a concrete situation?", to carry the reader into the second half.

## Close with an ASCII Structure Diagram — Form Follows Skeleton

Just naming "these generators" isn't enough — you need to **draw out the relationships between them**.

But **there's more than one way to draw it**. Whatever shape the root rank takes, the frame should take that same shape. Before drawing, ask first: **what shape is this rank's relationship, in itself?** Answer that, then pick the drawing method. **Form follows the skeleton, not the other way around.**

Nine families of frames — this table is the master index from skeleton to drawing method:

| Root rank shape | Nature of the relationship | Frame |
|---|---|---|
| Several standing side by side, independent, slidable | Orthogonal | Two-axis / multi-axis coordinate system |
| Layer holding up layer (deepest one is the meta-premise) | Nested penetration | Borehole profile |
| A single line pulled at both ends | Tension / opposition | Spectrum / slider |
| Pushing each other positively/negatively | Feedback loop | Loop diagram (mark +/-) |
| Segment after segment | Staged progression | Chain / steps |
| One splitting into many, splitting further | Hierarchical branching | Tree diagram |
| Many-to-many mutual hooking | Coupled network | Network diagram |
| Rising and falling, alternating rhythm | Oscillation | Waveform / oscillation curve |
| Multi-dimensional classification (e.g., abstraction level x distance x time) | Multi-dimensional classification | N-axis / multi-slice |

**Hard constraint: use only pure ASCII characters.** No Unicode symbols allowed (including arrows → ← ↑ ↓, boxes ┌─┐└┘├┤│, dots • ◆ ●, bold arrows ▶ ◀, etc.).

Allowed character set: letters, digits, spaces, and `- = | + * / \ < > ^ v [ ] ( ) { } . , : ; _ #`.

Substitution table (left disallowed / right replacement):

- `┌ ┐ └ ┘ ├ ┤ ┬ ┴ ┼` -> `+`
- `─ ━` -> `-` (or `=` for a heavy line)
- `│ ┃` -> `|`
- `→ ▶` -> `->`
- `← ◀` -> `<-`
- `↑` -> `^` or `|^`
- `↓` -> `v` or `|v`
- `◀─▶` -> `<->` or `<--->`
- `●` -> `*` or `o`
- `×` -> `x`

Below are nine worked examples — each paired with a minimal usable ASCII diagram plus one line of **demonstration requirements**. Which one to pick follows the shape of the root rank that the process above dug out.

### 1. Two-Axis / Multi-Axis Coordinate System

Fits: when the root rank's lines are orthogonal, independent, and slidable. Fritz's rocket geometry (vision clarity x current-reality honesty) is this kind.

**Demonstration requirement**: the diagram must mark the coordinates of several concrete phenomena — e.g., where "a hug" falls, where "a greeting" falls, where "a local reversal of entropy" falls. Only when the reader sees at a glance how the frame is used can they actually learn it. Axes with no example points are just an empty prop.

**Coordinate systems come in two tiers, which decide what to draw in each quadrant**:

- *Attribute-type coordinate system* — the two axes are categorical or state attributes (e.g., light-heavy x emotional-rational, extrovert-introvert x intuitive-perceiving). Each quadrant is just a category label; there's no dynamics happening within the cell. Just follow the requirement above — mark example coordinates and examples at the corners, no further drawing inside the cells.

- *Dynamics-type coordinate system* — the two axes are dynamical dimensions, and the system in each quadrant evolves a distinct temporal behavior. Like potential functions (conservative-dissipative x single-multiple minima), phase transitions (temperature-pressure), or games (zero-sum vs. non-zero-sum x one-shot vs. repeated). Flatly "labeling" this kind of coordinate system is wasteful — each quadrant should be upgraded into a **miniature ASCII picture framed in a rectangle**, drawing "how the system moves in this cell" directly inside the frame.

The test in one line: **does the system in this cell have a motion that can actually be drawn?** If yes — upgrade to a dynamics picture; if no — fall back to an attribute label.

**How to draw the dynamics picture**:
- Each quadrant is a rectangular frame (bordered with `+ - |`, like a small framed ASCII picture)
- Top of the frame draws "a miniature snapshot of this cell's dynamics" — terrain / state / arrows / a ball / a curve, your choice, pure ASCII
- Middle of the frame, one line describing "what the system is doing in this cell"
- Bottom of the frame marks the quadrant's label + 1-2 typical examples
- The four rectangles are separated by the coordinate axes (the axis lines run between the rectangles)
- Pixel-level alignment isn't required (unavoidable given character-width issues), but the content structure inside each frame should be consistent

Potential-function coordinate system example:

```
                                       Single minimum
                                         |^
                                         |
                                         |
   +----------------------------+        |        +----------------------------+
   |                            |        |        |                            |
   |         \________/         |        |        |         \________/         |
   |            o <->           |        |        |            o .             |
   |                            |        |        |                            |
   | single valley, ball        |        |        | single valley, ball        |
   | oscillates forever         |        |        | decelerates to rest        |
   |                            |        |        |                            |
   | conservative + single min  |        |        | dissipative + single min   |
   | [pendulum / oscillator]    |        |        | [convex optimization/SVM]  |
   +----------------------------+        |        +----------------------------+
                                         |
   conservative <----------------------+----------------------> dissipative
   (energy conserved)                    |     (energy drained by environment)
                                         |
   +----------------------------+        |        +----------------------------+
   |                            |        |        |                            |
   |    \_/\_/\_/\_/\_/         |        |        |    \_/\_/\_/\_/\_/         |
```

The reader sees at a glance: in each cell, "terrain + the ball's fate" is framed as a picture, and how the system evolves is directly visible. This is something a category label can never do.

### 2. Borehole Profile

Fits: when the root rank is layer holding up layer, the deepest one being the meta-premise. The reader passes straight down the vertical direction.

```
+------------------------------------+
| Surface layer: phenomenon           |
| [user opens the app every day]      |
+------------------------------------+
                 |
                 v
+------------------------------------+
| Middle layer: mechanism             |
| [push notifications + feedback +    |
|  habit loop]                        |
+------------------------------------+
                 |
                 v
+------------------------------------+
| Bottom layer: meta-premise          |
| [dopamine intermittent              |
|  reinforcement]                     |
+------------------------------------+
```

**Demonstration requirement**: every layer must be marked with a concrete example, so the reader can see the penetration path "phenomenon -> mechanism -> meta-premise" going straight down. The deepest layer is the meta-premise — don't leave it blank; leaving it blank means you never drilled to the bottom.

### 3. Spectrum / Slider

Fits: when the root rank is a single line being pulled at both ends, with cases falling somewhere in the middle.

```
Extreme A <===================o===================> Extreme B
[traits leaning toward A]     ^          [traits leaning toward B]
                              |
                        case X falls here
```

**Demonstration requirement**: mark the traits at both ends, and place at least one concrete case somewhere in the middle. Two ends with no case point = an empty prop — the reader needs to see how this ruler actually measures something.

### 4. Feedback Loop

Fits: when the root rank is a loop of generators pushing each other; the positive/negative directions must be drawn.

```
        A --(+)--> B
        ^          |
        |          |
       (-)        (+)
        |          |
        |          v
        D <--(+)-- C
```

**Demonstration requirement**: every arrow must be marked `+` or `-`; note beside the diagram the overall effect of the reinforcing loop (self-amplifying) and the balancing loop (self-dampening) — a loop diagram without +/- marks is the same as not drawing one.

### 5. Chain / Steps

Fits: when the root rank progresses in stages, one segment after another in time or logic.

Chain, laid out horizontally:

```
A ---> B ---> C ---> D
[stage name] [stage name] [stage name] [stage name]
```

Steps, laid out vertically (rising step by step):

```
                 +----+
                 | D  |
            +----+
            | C  |
       +----+
       | B  |
  +----+
  | A  |
  +----+
```

**Demonstration requirement**: name every stage, and mark the transition conditions (what pushes A to B, what raises C to D). A chain without transition conditions is just a listing, not a mechanism.

### 6. Tree Diagram

Fits: when the root rank has one generator splitting into several, which split further.

```
              root rank
              /   |   \
             A    B    C
            / \   |   /|\
           a   b  d  x y z
```

**Demonstration requirement**: name the classification principle at every branch point (by what criterion the layer above splits into the layer below) — a tree without named classification principles is a blur.

### 7. Network Diagram

Fits: when the root rank is many-to-many mutual hooking, with no hierarchy.

```
   A --------- B
   |\         /|
   | \       / |
   |  \     /  |
   |   \   /   |
   |    \ /    |
   |     X     |
   |    / \    |
   |   /   \   |
   |  /     \  |
   | /       \ |
   D --------- C
```

**Demonstration requirement**: every edge should carry a direction (if directional) and a strength (heavy line `=` / thin line `-`), and key nodes should be labeled. A tangle of connections with no primary/secondary distinction = a net, but not a skeleton.

### 8. Waveform / Oscillation Curve

Fits: when the root rank rises and falls, alternating in rhythm (e.g., seasons, cycles, boom-bust, focus-drift).

```
   /\          /\          /\
  /  \        /  \        /  \
 /    \      /    \      /    \
/      \____/      \____/      \____
^                    ^
trough               peak
[behavior at trough] [behavior at peak]
```

**Demonstration requirement**: mark at least one trough-peak pair with a concrete example, so the reader sees the states at different positions in the cycle — a waveform without marked positions is just a decorative line.

### 9. N-Axis / Multi-Slice

Fits: multi-dimensional classification with more than two axes (e.g., Y abstraction level x X distance x Z time). 3D is hard to draw clearly, so draw pairwise 2D slices:

```
Slice 1: abstraction x distance   Slice 2: abstraction x time
       high abstraction                  high abstraction
         ^                              ^
         |                              |
         |                              |
near <---+---> far              past <--+--> present
         |                              |
         |                              |
         v                              v
       low abstraction                  low abstraction
```

**Demonstration requirement**: mark at least one case's quadrant on every slice; only when every pairwise slice is drawn is it complete. Missing a pair = leaving a blind spot.

---

**Stacked style (root rank + frame): draw two diagrams.** The first is the root rank's skeleton (choose one of the nine above); the second is the frame layered onto one of its lines (with example coordinates / example points / example paths marked).
